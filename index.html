AI Reliability: What Breaks When an LLM Does Real Financial Work

Controlled study · findings

Before building an AI benchmarking workflow, I ran a controlled study of its riskiest step: whether a model could reliably identify the right peer companies for a financial benchmark. I tested it against sets the team already trusted as ground truth, across several sectors, scoring agreement across repeated runs with 'Reference saved memories' and 'Reference chat history' both switched off, so each run was independent and the results were clean and reproducible.

The core finding, about how the model chose peer companies, turned out to be a question about alignment rather than capability. On the hardest case the model scored near zero at first, but not because it was incapable: it was quietly answering a slightly different question than the one we were asking, selecting sensible peers under its own implicit definition of "comparable" rather than ours. Writing our actual screening criteria into the prompt lifted agreement on that case from near-zero to about 68%, and the size of the lift was inversely related to how well the model did unaided: the messier and more niche the sector, the more the criteria mattered. The remaining ceiling was definitional, not a capability limit.

Stress-testing the workflow, I independently ran into, diagnosed, and fixed six distinct failure modes that map onto core problems in AI evaluation and alignment:

  • Specification gaming: the model optimised a proxy count instead of the real objective, dropping genuinely core peers to hit a target number.
  • Benchmark contamination: an evaluation metric flattered the model because the ground-truth answer sat inside the candidate pool it was scoring against.
  • Overfitting: I caught myself reverse-engineering keywords from the known answers, which would have proved nothing, and stopped.
  • Measurement artefact: the model's "wrong" picks were actually more correct; the scoring rule itself was wrong, not the model.
  • Objective-vs-proxy misspecification: a similarity proxy that worked in one sector silently failed in another, so I had to rewrite it around what really made firms comparable.
  • Limits of formalisation: some judgments of "who is truly comparable" could not be fully written into rules, which is exactly where a human checkpoint belongs.

The through-line: every case was a gap between the true objective and an operable proxy, and a system faithfully optimising the proxy drifts from the objective. That gap is the heart of the alignment problem, and I met it from the concrete side, in real work, before I had the vocabulary for it.

I turned the findings into a hybrid architecture: a deterministic screen supplies the candidate pool, the model selects and explains within it, a person signs off, and the financial calculations run in plain Python with no model involved, so accuracy never depends on the model behaving. I built the prototype for the deterministic core, presented the findings to the firm's CTO, and secured sponsorship to lead the build.

Governed AI on a High-Stakes Government Bid

Applied · governance

On a mid eight figure UK government infrastructure bid, on a two-week timeline, a senior MD asked me to raise the quality of our technical response using AI. From several dozen tender documents I stood up a document-grounded research base, built an operating-model comparison matrix across countries, and ran competitor and pressure-point analysis mapped to each question.

What mattered was how I did it: with guardrails. I ran a governed, source-controlled workflow with a full audit trail and source-coverage checks, so every claim was traceable to a document and unsupported content was caught before it reached a reviewer. I used chain-of-thought prompting to force the model to justify each judgement, for example why a competitor would position itself a certain way, so its reasoning stayed visible and checkable rather than a black-box conclusion. The senior team built their revisions around that output and delivered review-ready responses inside the deadline.

First bid of this scale · governed, source-traceable AI · MD-review-ready in two weeks

AI Workflow Architecture for an Advisory Firm

Design · adopted

On my own initiative, I built a mental model of how AI should work at the firm, not task-level tools but governed, workflow-level systems, including the distinction between agentic and AI-augmented workflows, where human checkpoints belong, and how client data stays compliant. I presented it to the CTO, drove consensus, and secured sponsorship to lead the build. Separately, a prompt-engineering framework I designed, with confidence tagging and source-citation rules built in as quality controls, was adopted as a practice-wide standard.
